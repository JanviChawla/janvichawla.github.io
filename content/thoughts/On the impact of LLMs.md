## On Reading

Certain writing, as you're reading, breaks the fourth wall. It takes out of being immersed in the content to then being engaged with the meta (much like this is doing right now). If you are reading a resume or reviewing a client presentation, let's say you come across a typo or an orphan word. If you're someone who knows to look out for those moments, it takes you out of the experience. I see these as mistakes - they ruin the sort of perfection, the facade that's been stood up. The white glove delivery that was promised when you decided to look at this polish-promised piece.

Writing and reading through LLMs has a similar outcome. As I'm reading, if I come across an em dash, it takes me out of the reading environment and puts me in a wary state wondering, can I trust this content?

There are certain known tells that make me question the writing: groups of 3s, em dashes, and "it's not *x*, it's *y*." I don't know yet how to feel about it, because I assume that the more people publish content, emails, even IMs using LLMs, the more it's going to be okay that all of us are using it - and that this is how writing just is now.

There are silver linings. One positive could be bringing stylistic choices like the em dash back into favor and popular use. Are people using it correctly? Most likely yes, if they're using ChatGPT or similar to create this content. I've oscillated on whether to continue using em dashes in my writing - I use them more frequently than the average person - so as to make the reader feel this is a safe, AI-free space. But to fight against this would fight against my authentic voice and more importantly, a perfectly appropriate use of punctuation when used correctly.

Does it benefit your content to look like it's human-written, if it's all still saying essentially the same thing - perhaps even more eloquently forming your thoughts for you? I've seen videos on YouTube touting themselves as "human-generated music" (not on my bingo card, but then again, many phenomena over the past five years have been unprecedented times); while it may be click-bait, it certainly works.

## On Speaking

It feels like we're all turning into pop stars who lip-sync and auto-tune more than we sing live. That's worrisome, because conversation is something that is not only prepped for; it's constant, it's impromptu. 

How can we keep our edge at work while we're being promoted, encouraged to use AI tools? If we delegate and outsource too much of our cognitive load away, while beneficial to some extent to get work done faster, it leaves us hanging in unplanned-for situations. Many jobs require being able to think on the spot, to adjust to a changing or unknown chain of topics and conversation. That's what consulting, for example, is known for - responding in real time. 

If we start to rely too heavily on LLMs, must we put up guardrails around when and where we use them? Do we only use it for the brainstorming versus the final editing? There's also the question of sameness. Do we re-edit our writing to make it more human and give it our fingerprints? I'm afraid we will start sounding all more homogeneous as a result of continuously using it and reading similarly-worded outcomes. I also worry about those who haven't yet even found their voice, who are too young and already enveloped in a digital world, living in self-constructed fishbowls. Will it flatten their uniqueness and stop the forming of more creative people? Perhaps that's too pessimistic a point of view...

## On Thinking

So how do we keep the edge - that ability to think sharply, speak clearly, and operate without a script - while still benefiting from the tools we have now? Here's what I've started to explore and practices I've considered implementing more regularly:

1. **Use AI to sharpen, not substitute.** LLMs can help shape your thinking, but it shouldn't *be* your thinking. I draft the slide, paragraph, or outline before turning to models and use the tool instead to tighten, stress test, or reframe what I've already started. I fear not forming my own POV if I start with ChatGPT from the outset.
2. **Practice unscripted thinking.** My job involves impromptu conversations, client meetings, leadership discussions - letting those muscles atrophy puts me in a dire spot. I plan to build "live response" drills into my week: answering a big question out loud in one minute, rephrasing an abstract ask into a tangible task. Talking through a deck without looking at the slides. Practicing without preparation to keep me on my toes. I anyways find it difficult to remember all that ChatGPT has given me to work with, so it's still important to practice my own thinking and speaking (obviously).
3. **Don't take LLM output as gospel.** There was a point where I would let ChatGPT have the final word. Whatever it said, I believed it came from an informed place, from having consumed good writing and valid research, as it practically knew the entire contents of the internet. Instead of accepting a first answer as the finished product, I've started to question and push back the more I use it - a helpful exercise.
4. **Do the hard thing.** It's tempting to skip the messy middle, the sausage-making. It's easy to go as a first resort to LLMs and have it answer a question within seconds before you've formed your own POV. This ties to point one but is more about sitting in strategic thinking purposefully - it requires time, and we as a society only want things faster. Fight that urge where it makes sense. In a world where more of our work can be delegated, the real value comes from knowing when and what not to delegate.
5. **Journal.** Write what comes to mind freely. Write uncensored. Write like no one's reading because no one is (if you are, hi!). Mind-map different parts of your life to make connections across pillars. Sit in reflection. Let your thoughts be your thoughts, without outside input from Chat.
6. **Read.** Don't let LLM outputs be the primary material you consume. There were writers and readers long before AI-generated content - good work, too. That's what it's all trained on to begin with. Be aware of what you put in, of tells. Expand your vocabulary and the diversity of what goes in your mind by reading different types of content.

If you have any tips or practices you implement to keep your edge, let me know!
